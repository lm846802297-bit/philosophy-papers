# The Ideological Fetishism of Formal Verification: A Žižekian Critique of the Lean-Verification Credential in Mathematical Knowledge Production

**Project:** *In Lean We Trust?*  
**Version:** Revised research draft after anonymous referee report  
**Evidence cut-off:** 28 November 2026  
**Status:** Conceptual comparative study; the central diagnosis is proposed and operationalized, not presumed to hold in every case.

## Abstract

Formal verification provides a genuine but bounded form of mathematical warrant: Lean checks whether a proof term has the required type for a specified formal proposition in a specified environment. Yet the public credential “Lean-verified” may circulate beyond that local result and acquire broader epistemic authority. This article asks when such an enlargement performs an ideological function in mathematical knowledge production. Drawing on Žižek’s critique of ideology, it develops the concept of the ideological fetishism of formal verification. Ideological fantasy stages closure around wider uncertainty; fetishism describes the displacement through which authority generated by technical, semantic, and institutional relations appears to reside autonomously in the credential; fetishistic disavowal remains a secondary mechanism requiring evidence that acknowledged limits are practically subordinated to a broader guarantee. Taking existing component-based accounts of mathematical warrant as its technical baseline, the article proposes a three-part diagnostic: warrant-scope transfer, objectal displacement, and the social efficacy of that alleged transfer. A comparative documentary analysis examines four cases: the Riemann hypothesis, Navier–Stokes, Erdős problems investigated with AlphaProof Nexus, and the Liquid Tensor Experiment. None establishes the full diagnostic conjunction. The cases instead show distinct configurations of institutional certification, specification disputes, distributed attribution, and semantic auditability. The article offers a conditional Žižekian framework for distinguishing rational reliance on formal verification from the ideological enlargement of its genuine technical warrant.

**Keywords:** philosophy of science; philosophy of mathematical practice; social epistemology; Slavoj Žižek; ideology critique; formal verification

## 1. Introduction: What Is Ideological About a Correct Proof?

What, precisely, could be ideological about a correct proof?

The question appears paradoxical. If Lean accepts a proof term for a formal proposition, the system has performed the task for which it was designed. Its acceptance is not a matter of persuasion or rhetorical confidence. Under a specified environment, the kernel checks whether the proof term has the required type. Why, then, introduce the language of ideology? Would this not confuse a genuine mathematical achievement with a social illusion?

The objection assumes that ideology is primarily a false representation of reality: an erroneous belief that disappears once the relevant facts are supplied. Žižek’s critique of ideology challenges this reduction. The ideological function of a claim cannot be inferred simply from whether its explicit content is true or false. A factually correct proposition may participate in an ideological relation through the position it occupies and the practice it helps sustain (Žižek 1989; 1994; Flisfeder 2023). The question is therefore not whether Lean’s verdict is correct, but what the credential “Lean-verified” is made to authorize.

Formal verification is a revealing object because its technical achievement is real and delimited. Lean checks a proof term against a formal proposition in a formal environment. That operation does not, by itself, settle every relation between the formal proposition and the intended mathematical claim. Nor does kernel acceptance alone determine the theorem’s significance, the adequacy of its natural-language exposition, or the institutional status of the result. These are distinct components of the warrant architecture through which a formal result becomes a mathematical claim. This distinction is not a criticism of Lean; it identifies the different epistemic tasks performed by formal checking, semantic interpretation, mathematical exposition, and institutional assessment.

The philosophical problem arises when a credential that warrants one component of this architecture is made to stand for the whole. A machine-checked proof can become a portable sign of certainty: a compact, reproducible result that researchers, institutions, and communicators can cite without reconstructing every stage of the underlying work. Such reliance may be rational and epistemically justified. The question is when the credential ceases to function as a properly scoped component of a wider warrant and begins to appear as an autonomous guarantee of the wider claim.

The paradox at the centre of this article is that the machine need not be unreliable for its authority to acquire an ideological function. Its genuine reliability may make the symbolic investment possible. A weak instrument could hardly serve as a compelling guarantee. The ideological question begins precisely where the technical achievement is real, but the authority attached to it may exceed the task it performs.

This article develops the concept of the **ideological fetishism of formal verification**. Its central question is:

> Under what conditions does the genuine but bounded warrant of Lean verification acquire an ideological function as an apparently self-sufficient guarantee of broader mathematical authority?

The proposed concept concerns the public and institutional credential “Lean-verified,” not Lean software as such and not every instance of formal proof. It identifies a possible transformation through which authority generated by a network of technical, semantic, and institutional relations appears to reside autonomously in the verification credential. The analysis distinguishes three moments that must not be conflated: the extension of the credential’s warrant beyond the formal proposition; the displacement of the relations that support the wider authority; and the practical or institutional efficacy of that displacement. A public statement can be inaccurate without being fetishistic; an institution can recognize a result without displacing its supporting procedures; and technical reuse can occur without reliance on the theorem as a mathematical premise.

Three Žižekian concepts organize the analysis. **Ideological fantasy** concerns the scene through which a local check may appear to close a wider uncertainty. **Fetishism** concerns the objectal displacement through which authority generated by a network of relations appears to reside in the credential itself. **Fetishistic disavowal** concerns the possible persistence of practical investment after relevant limits have been acknowledged. It is treated as a secondary and higher-evidence mechanism, not as a necessary feature of every case. Žižek’s account of practical belief is especially important: belief need not take the form of an explicit proposition in an individual’s mind; it can be externalized in objects, procedures, and repeated social practices. This insight directs the inquiry toward documented practices rather than speculative claims about private motives.

The article also uses Lacanian concepts to analyze the symbolic conditions of authorization. The Big Other names the symbolic locus of norms and recognition, not an empirical institution or software system. The subject-supposed-to-know names a position attributed to a subject within a transferential relation; it is not a synonym for trust in a tool. The analysis therefore asks who is publicly positioned as knowing—the expert, the mathematician, the AI solver, or another participant—and how that position is supported by formal credentials and institutional practices. It does not infer a psychoanalytic transference relation merely from the use of AI or proof assistants.

The article makes a deliberately bounded contribution. It does not claim to be the first Žižekian critique of technology, the first social epistemology of proof assistants, or the first account of the distinction between formal validity and semantic fidelity. Recent work has developed component-based accounts of mathematical warrant, examined the shift from derivational verification to human adjudication, analyzed distributed human-machine mathematical labour, and investigated ideological functions of AI systems (Vallverdú 2026; Kallel and El Louadi 2026; López-Corona 2026; Erfani 2026). Earlier studies have also examined the social certification of mathematical and logical proofs (De Millo, Lipton, and Perlis 1979; MacKenzie 1998; Rosental 2003). The present article takes these contributions as interlocutors. Its narrower contribution is a Žižekian analysis of how the credential “Lean-verified” may become an apparently autonomous bearer of authority beyond the formal warrant it supplies, and a diagnostic distinction between that possible ideological operation and rational, transparently mediated reliance.

The argument is tested against four cases with different production histories and institutional settings: the Riemann hypothesis, Navier–Stokes, Erdős problems investigated with AlphaProof Nexus, and the Liquid Tensor Experiment. These cases are not treated as four equivalent examples of ideological fetishism. They are heterogeneous cases selected to test the conditions under which formal credentials are authorized, contested, translated, reused, or kept within visible networks of semantic mediation. The analysis distinguishes institutional recognition from the social efficacy of an alleged warrant-scope transfer, technical integration from theorem-level reliance, and achievement-label compression from an enlargement of proof warrant. It does not infer audience beliefs or unconscious motives from public texts.

The article proceeds as follows. Section 2 specifies the technical and epistemic object, drawing on existing warrant-architecture literature. Section 3 reconstructs the relevant elements of Žižek’s critique of ideology. Section 4 defines the ideological fetishism of formal verification and establishes diagnostic requirements. Section 5 explains the comparative method. Section 6 examines the four Lean cases. Section 7 compares the cases and evaluates the originality of the proposed concept. Section 8 concludes.

## 2. What Does a Lean Verification Credential Warrant?

### 2.1 Kernel acceptance and the formal object

Lean is a proof assistant based on dependent type theory. A proposition is represented as a formal type, and a proof is represented by a term inhabiting that type. The kernel checks whether the term has the required type relative to the formal environment, definitions, axioms, imported libraries, and trusted implementation.

The core technical claim can be represented schematically as:

\[
\operatorname{KernelAccepts}(t : P \mid \Gamma),
\]

where \(t\) is a proof term, \(P\) is the formal proposition, and \(\Gamma\) is the relevant formal environment. Acceptance provides a genuine and reproducible form of derivational warrant: the proof term is accepted as establishing \(P\) under \(\Gamma\).

This warrant is substantial. It can exclude many classes of derivational error, permit replay of proof artifacts, and support independent checking by separately implemented kernels or proof checkers. The present article does not treat this reliability as illusory. Nor does it assume that an opaque process of proof discovery undermines the warrant supplied by a transparent checker. Duede and Davey (2025) argue that a transparent proof checker can provide genuine a priori mathematical knowledge even when an opaque AI system generated the proof. Their argument is an important counterweight to any attempt to reduce machine verification to a merely symbolic performance.

The relevant distinction is between the formal proposition that the kernel checks and the intended mathematical claim to which that proposition is supposed to correspond. Kernel acceptance establishes the former under the specified environment. It does not independently certify every step by which the intended claim was selected, represented, interpreted, and publicly characterized.

### 2.2 Warrant architecture and the specification relation

The present article does not introduce a new taxonomy of proof-level warrant. Vallverdú’s Locus Thesis argues that properties such as reliability, surveyability, transferability, explanatory power, and epistemic significance belong primarily to components of a warrant architecture, and that properties of the whole are licensed only by explicit composition rules (Vallverdú 2026). His architecture distinguishes the intended claim, its object-level specification, the justificatory object, the checking procedure, the trusted base, the error profile, the allocation of epistemic functions, replication routes, and the selection profile.

Kallel and El Louadi (2026) similarly distinguish derivational validity, representational fidelity, and epistemic significance. They argue that abundant machine checking does not remove the need for qualified human adjudication; it can shift the burden toward the relation between a formal statement and the intended mathematical question.

These accounts establish a necessary baseline for the present argument. Let \(P\) denote an intended mathematical claim and \(P^*\) the formal proposition submitted to Lean. The kernel checks a proof object for \(P^*\). The relation

\[
P \longleftrightarrow P^*
\]

is a specification relation: it concerns whether the formal proposition captures the intended mathematical claim. The kernel does not, simply by checking a proof of \(P^*\), certify this relation. It can be investigated through mathematical interpretation, source comparison, formal specification review, independent expert scrutiny, and, where possible, additional formalization of the specification relation itself.

The point is not that semantic correspondence is impossible to establish. It can be assessed rigorously, and formal methods can assist that assessment. The point is that the acceptance of a proof term and the fidelity of the specification are different epistemic tasks. If the specification is itself formalized, the warrant architecture becomes more explicit; it does not follow that every interpretive and institutional question has thereby disappeared.

### 2.3 Rational dependence is not ideological fetishism

Scientific and mathematical knowledge depend on a division of epistemic labour. Hardwig’s account of epistemic dependence explains why it can be rational to rely on experts whose evidence or competence one cannot personally reproduce (Hardwig 1985). De Millo, Lipton, and Perlis (1979) likewise emphasize the social processes through which proofs are communicated, criticized, and accepted. The fact that a mathematician relies on Lean, Mathlib maintainers, independent checkers, or expert reviewers does not by itself indicate ideology.

The same applies to institutions. A mathematical community may reasonably treat a proof-checking result as strong evidence for derivational validity. A journal may reasonably require a reproducible formal artifact. A reader may rationally rely on an expert’s report that the formal statement matches the intended theorem. These are ordinary forms of epistemic dependence.

The distinction matters because the article does not equate social mediation with ideology. All complex knowledge is socially mediated in some way. A Žižekian diagnosis requires more: the social and semantic relations that make the credential meaningful must be displaced into the apparent autonomous authority of the credential, and this appearance must perform a legitimating function beyond the credential’s actual warrant.

## 3. A Žižekian Critique of the Verification Credential

### 3.1 Ideology beyond false consciousness

In “The Spectre of Ideology,” Žižek challenges the assumption that ideology can be identified simply by the falsity of explicit beliefs. The ideological function of a position depends not only on its propositional content but also on the position it occupies within a social practice (Žižek 1994). A statement can be factually accurate and still participate in an ideological relation. This does not mean that every true statement is ideological, or that truth is reducible to power. It means that factual accuracy alone does not settle the question of ideological function.

Applied to formal verification, the statement “Lean checked this proof” may be entirely accurate. The question is what that statement is made to authorize. Does it refer to the exact formal proposition and the environment in which it was checked? Or is the credential made to stand for the correctness, significance, or final acceptance of a wider mathematical claim without the relevant specification and interpretive relations being established?

Four phenomena must remain distinct:

1. **Factual accuracy:** whether the statement about Lean’s checking is true.
2. **Rhetorical compression:** whether a short description omits technical detail while remaining accurate in context.
3. **Institutional recognition:** whether a recognized community or institution confers status on the result.
4. **Ideological function:** whether the credential is made to appear as an autonomous guarantee of a wider claim, thereby displacing the relations on which its warrant depends.

These phenomena can overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A press release is not ideological merely because it is short. Institutional recognition is not fetishistic merely because it carries authority. The concept developed here applies only where a more specific objectal and legitimating operation can be shown.

### 3.2 Ideological fantasy: the scene of closure

In The Sublime Object of Ideology, Žižek’s account of fantasy goes beyond the idea that ideology is a false image concealing an otherwise complete reality. Fantasy provides coordinates through which social reality is experienced and helps sustain the appearance of coherence where a constitutive gap remains (Žižek 1989, 28–31).

The relevant gap in formal verification is not a defect in the kernel. It is the non-identity between the formal object that is checked and the intended mathematical claim to which it is connected. This relation can be rigorously audited, but it is not established by the same operation that checks the proof term. The wider warrant also depends on assumptions, definitions, provenance, mathematical interpretation, and institutional evaluation.

The fantasy of formal verification arises when the kernel’s local verdict is staged as the point at which this wider chain of uncertainty has been closed. The certificate appears to settle not only whether \\(t\\) proves \\(P^*\\), but also whether \\(P^*\\) is the intended claim, whether the result has the significance attributed to it, and whether the relevant mathematical questions are settled.

The distinction can be represented as:

\[
\operatorname{KernelAccepts}(t : P^* \mid \Gamma)
\]

which is a technical result, whereas

\[
\operatorname{KernelAccepts}(t : P^* \mid \Gamma)
\Rightarrow
\text{the wider mathematical claim is fully settled}
\]

is not a consequence of kernel acceptance alone.

The fantasy is not necessarily an explicit belief that Lean verifies empirical reality or certifies every mathematical interpretation. It is a possible symbolic organization of the verification event: the local check becomes the visible scene in which the broader demand for certainty appears to have been satisfied. The fantasy does not eliminate the specification relation; it makes the work of that relation appear already handled.

This distinction also prevents an overextension of Žižek. Not every use of a certificate stages closure. A source that identifies the formal proposition, trusted base, semantic review, human contributions, and limits of the result may resist the fantasy by keeping the different warrant components visible.

### 3.3 Fetishism: the credential as objectal bearer of authority

Marx’s account of commodity fetishism concerns the way relations among producers assume the appearance of relations among things (Marx 1976 [1867], vol. I, ch. 1, §4). Žižek emphasizes the form through which an effect generated by a network of relations can appear to reside autonomously in an object (Žižek 1989, 19–20).

The present article proposes a structural extension of this insight to the **Lean-verification credential**. The object of analysis is not Lean software in itself. It is the credential “Lean-verified” as it circulates in research papers, institutional announcements, repositories, and public accounts.

The credential depends on a network that includes:
- selection and formulation of the intended theorem;
- formal specification and definitions;
- proof object and its dependencies;
- Lean’s kernel, libraries, and trusted environment;
- software and library maintenance;
- statement comparison and semantic scrutiny;
- expert assessment and institutional recognition;
- public description of what the result establishes.

These elements perform different epistemic functions. Collective labour does not create mathematical truth in the same way that labour produces commodities. It produces, maintains, checks, interprets, and authorizes the artifacts and relations through which a formal result is connected to an intended mathematical claim.

Thomas, Nafus, and Sherman (2018) offer a close precedent. Drawing on ethnographic work with computer-vision professionals and the Quantified Self community, they analyze algorithms as material objects that stabilize social relations and carry promises generated through exchanges among differently positioned makers and users. Their account explicitly traces the slippage from technical capabilities to broader claims and treats the resulting investment as socially productive rather than as simple false consciousness. The present article builds on that insight; it does not claim to introduce algorithmic fetishism or the general idea that technical objects can materialize social relations. Its object is narrower: the public and institutional credential “Lean-verified,” whose authority begins in a precise, genuine operation—kernel acceptance of a proof term for a specified proposition under a specified environment. The distinctive question is how this local warrant is attributed to a broader mathematical claim without an adequately supported specification and interpretive bridge. The S/O/E diagnostic separates that scope transfer, the apparent residence of authority in the credential, and the documented efficacy of the transfer. The contribution is therefore a domain-specific diagnostic for the ideological enlargement of a formal-verification credential, not a general theory of algorithmic fetishism.

The fetishistic hypothesis arises when the authority generated through this network appears to reside autonomously in the credential itself. The credential then seems to contain the guarantee, rather than to certify one component of a wider architecture. This is more specific than the omission of contributors from a short announcement. Omission matters only when it contributes to a representation or practice in which the credential is treated as self-sufficient and the relations necessary to interpret its scope are displaced.

The concept can therefore be defined as follows:

> **The ideological fetishism of formal verification is the objectal displacement through which the socially and semantically mediated authority of a machine-checked result appears to reside autonomously in the credential “Lean-verified,” allowing the warrant of a bounded formal check to stand for a wider mathematical claim.**

This is a proposed Žižekian extension, not a term coined by Žižek. The analogy with commodity fetishism is structural rather than identical: the article does not claim that Lean is a commodity in Marx’s technical economic sense, or that mathematical validity is produced by social labour.

### 3.4 Fetishistic disavowal: a secondary and conditional mechanism

Žižek’s discussion of cynical ideology draws on Sloterdijk’s critique of cynical reason (Sloterdijk 1987; Žižek 1989, ch. 1). The formula “I know very well, but nevertheless” is a shorthand for a particular structure of disavowal; it should not be treated as a definition of every form of disavowal. More important for this article is Žižek’s account of fetishistic belief as a practical relation. Subjects may know in theory that money is an expression of social relations while, in their social activity, treating it as the immediate embodiment of wealth. Žižek describes them as “fetishists in practice, not in theory”; belief is externalized in the object and in effective social procedures (Žižek 1989, pp. 28–32). Scholarship distinguishes cynical disavowal from fetishistic disavowal (Kuldova 2019; Black 2025).

Disavowal is not a necessary condition of fetishism. A credential may perform an objectal and legitimating function without evidence that a particular actor explicitly acknowledged its limitations. Fetishistic disavowal is a stronger and separate claim: it requires an identifiable acknowledgement of the relevant limitation, continued practical investment in a broader guarantee, and evidence that the acknowledged gap is subordinated to the credential in a consequential practice. The belief at issue is not inferred from private psychology. It is located in the public or institutional practice through which the credential is treated as if it carried the wider guarantee.

The AlphaProof case provides a candidate for testing this mechanism, not a completed diagnosis: the Science paper reports expert validation of the formal statements and itemizes human contributions, while its public summary compresses nine heterogeneous formal statements into the claim of “nine Erdős problems” solved by the agent. Whether this is objectal displacement, conventional benchmark shorthand, or a scope-preserving description must be decided from the full communication chain and the exact variant-level artifacts. The Navier–Stokes case illustrates a different limit: independent criticism and continued technical reuse coexist, but that coexistence alone does not establish disavowal. Reuse may be limited to benchmarking, porting, auditing, or infrastructure integration rather than reliance on the theorem as a mathematical premise. Riemann’s reconstruction activity and LTE’s explicit semantic-audit practices provide further counter-patterns to any assumption that formal verification necessarily displaces interpretation.

The article therefore does not infer unconscious motives, cynical intent, or community-wide disavowal from public wording. The relevant empirical question is narrower: where an acknowledged gap remains, does a repeated practice continue to organize judgment, attribution, or institutional action as if the wider guarantee had already been secured?

### 3.5 The missing guarantee: the Big Other as background

The Lacanian/Žižekian idea that there is no complete Big Other and no “Other of the Other” provides a structural background to the argument (Lacan 2019, ch. XVI; Žižek 1989, Part II). No final symbolic authority can guarantee the consistency of the entire order from outside it. This does not imply that mathematical proof is impossible or that all standards are arbitrary.

Lean is not the Big Other. The Big Other names the symbolic locus of language, law, norms, and recognition, not an empirical institution. Neither Lean nor a journal, university, or research organization is itself the Big Other; each may be a site where symbolic rules and recognitions are enacted. Lean may be positioned as a technical credential within this symbolic order. The relevant question is whether the credential is made to function as if it supplied a final guarantee of every level of mathematical knowledge. The Big Other is therefore a supporting premise, not a separate empirical mechanism and not a fourth co-equal concept.

The subject-supposed-to-know is also distinct from the Big Other. In Seminar XI, it names a position of knowledge attributed to a subject within a transferential relation; it is not a generic synonym for trust in software or experts (Lacan 1977, pp. 230–35, especially p. 232). The concept can be used cautiously to examine when a mathematician, solver, expert, or institutional representative is installed in a knowing position. A Lean certificate is not itself the subject-supposed-to-know; at most, it can function as a signifier or credential through which such an attribution is supported or displaced. The paper does not diagnose the public as being in a literal psychoanalytic transference relation with Lean.

### 3.6 Recognition, credit, and the archive: supporting perspectives

Marx–Lacan–Žižek remain the explanatory core. Three supporting perspectives clarify empirical processes that the core framework does not need to absorb.

**Bourdieu and Merton: recognition and credit.** Bourdieu's account of the scientific field explains how scientific authority and scientific capital are produced through struggles over recognition, position, autonomy, and legitimate competence (Bourdieu 2004, pt. II, chs. 2–4). Merton's studies of priority and the Matthew effect explain how scientific institutions allocate credit and how recognition can accrue unevenly to already prominent contributors (Merton 1957; 1968). These approaches help analyze solver attribution, authorship, institutional status, and the separation of proof discovery from formalization, validation, maintenance, and exposition. They explain how recognition is distributed and converted into credit; they do not replace the Žižekian account of objectal displacement.

**Derrida: archive and repeatability.** Derrida links the archive's principle of commencement to commandment and describes archontic authority as involving custody, interpretation, classification, and consignation—the gathering of records into a unified corpus (Derrida 1996, pp. 1–4). This perspective is useful for versioned repositories, branch histories, pinned commits, contributor files, and proof archives: these are not simply passive memory stores, since versioning, access, and maintenance shape which artifacts remain retrievable and authoritative. The analogy is limited. A Git repository is not identical to the political or psychoanalytic archive Derrida analyzes, and the framework does not by itself explain why a preserved artifact acquires wider epistemic authority.

**Hardwig and the sociology of proof: rational dependence and material production.** Hardwig's account of epistemic dependence provides the normative baseline for justified reliance on expertise (Hardwig 1985, pp. 335–40). De Millo, Lipton, and Perlis (1979) analyze the social processes through which mathematical proofs become accepted; MacKenzie (1998) examines the relation between computers and mathematical proof; and Rosental (2003) ethnographically documents the production and certification of a logical theorem in AI through heterogeneous evaluation practices and interactions among a community of interlocutors. These studies establish that proof credibility and certification already have a social history. The present article does not claim to discover that proof is socially negotiated. Its narrower question concerns the symbolic function of the Lean-verification credential. These accounts, together with Hardwig, also prevent the analysis from treating every reliance on experts, repositories, or formal tools as ideological.

### 3.7 Post-verification circulation

The empirical comparison identifies five distinct modes through which a formal artifact circulates during and after its release into a research community:

1. **Credentialization:** a proof, solver, or formal statement receives status and is represented as a recognized result.
2. **Translation:** the formal artifact is reconstructed as a human-readable mathematical argument, exposition, or domain-specific interpretation.
3. **Integration:** code, declarations, dependencies, or proof artifacts enter another repository, benchmark, library, or toolchain.
4. **Assimilation:** the result becomes a premise, method, or object in subsequent mathematical research. The strongest form is theorem-level dependence in a later result.
5. **Archival stabilization:** version pins, source histories, contributor records, citations, and documentation make the artifact repeatable and retrievable.

These modes are analytically distinct and need not occur in a fixed order. Riemann exhibits strong credentialization, translation, and archival stabilization while theorem-level assimilation is not yet evidenced in the present source record. Navier–Stokes exhibits substantial integration and reproducibility work alongside a continuing dispute about paper-to-Lean correspondence. AlphaProof Nexus exhibits credentialization and distributed attribution; its separate quantum-graph result enters translation and further mathematical development, whereas the nine Erdős outputs have no located downstream theorem dependence. LTE exhibits methodological diffusion and related library infrastructure without direct reuse of its original Lean 3 theorem.

Existing software-citation scholarship already analyses how citation and dependency graphs provide context, traceability, reproducibility, trust, authority, and credit for software artifacts (Druskat 2020; Smith et al. 2016). The present fivefold scheme does not claim to introduce software citation, dependency graphs, or transitive credit. Its purpose is to distinguish, in formal mathematical cases, public credentialization and human translation from technical integration, theorem-level assimilation, and archival stabilization.

Post-verification circulation is a descriptive model, not a new philosophical theory and not a claim that every artifact passes through all five modes. These modes do not themselves establish warrant-scope transfer: technical integration may occur without theorem-level use, translation may increase understanding, and archival stabilization may increase reproducibility. Warrant-scope transfer must be established separately by showing that the credential's local warrant is publicly or institutionally attributed to a wider claim without an adequately supported bridge. The circulation model describes pathways; the S/O/E diagnostic evaluates when a particular pathway becomes an ideological conversion.

### 3.8 Three empirical planes

Žižek's “The Spectre of Ideology” distinguishes ideology as explicit doctrine, ideology in its material externality of practices and institutions, and the more elusive spontaneous operation of ideology in social reality (Žižek 1994, pp. 9–10). The present study operationalizes these as three empirical planes:

1. **Discursive plane:** papers, technical reports, institutional statements, press releases, and media accounts that describe what Lean verification establishes.
2. **Material-institutional plane:** repositories, proof-checking workflows, review procedures, publication practices, status registers, and institutional acts of recognition.
3. **Spontaneous-practical plane:** repeated practices in which a Lean credential is treated as a default warrant for claims broader than the formal proposition.

These planes are not additional theoretical mechanisms. The third plane requires evidence of repeated practice and cannot be inferred from a headline, a citation, a successful build, or technical artifact reuse alone. The Stage 3 corpus documents the first two planes extensively; it does not yet establish a repeated practice, within any of the four cases, of treating the credential as a comprehensive guarantee.

## 4. Diagnostic Model: From Warrant to Ideological Authority

### 4.1 The constitutive gap and its possible ideological management

The central problem is not that formal verification leaves everything uncertain. It resolves a specific class of uncertainty with unusual precision. The warrant of a formal check belongs to one component of a wider architecture. The relation between the intended claim and the formal specification, the provenance of the artifact, and the institutional interpretation of the result remain distinct tasks.

The empirical study therefore distinguishes six dimensions:
- kernel-level derivational validity;
- fidelity of the formal statement and definitions to the intended mathematics;
- human mathematical understanding and interpretation;
- institutional recognition and attribution;
- downstream artifact or methodological reuse;
- theorem-level mathematical dependence.

These dimensions can interact, but none is a substitute for another. A build or repository import establishes technical integration, not necessarily mathematical reliance. A citation can establish recognition or contextual use without establishing that the theorem is used as a premise. A human-readable reconstruction can advance understanding without yet being a complete independent proof.

### 4.2 Diagnostic requirements

The technical warrant is the baseline for analysis, not an indicator that distinguishes ideological from non-ideological use. For each case, the study identifies the exact proposition, proof artifact, environment, axioms, dependencies, and checking record.

A strong diagnosis of the ideological fetishism of formal verification requires three further conditions. The central operation tested here is **warrant-scope transfer**: a non-deductive movement in public or institutional attribution from the warrant that Lean supplies for a formal proposition to a broader intended mathematical claim, or to claims about its significance or settled status, without an adequately supported specification and interpretive bridge. This is not an inference performed by Lean. It is a transfer of epistemic authority in representation and practice.

- **S — Warrant-scope transfer (scope extension):** a public or institutional representation attributes to the Lean credential a warrant broader than the checked proposition and the documented semantic bridge establish. The exact inferential gap must be identified.
- **O — Objectal displacement:** the credential is represented or practically treated as a self-sufficient source of the wider authority, while the semantic, technical, or institutional relations that make it meaningful are backgrounded or displaced. Mere omission of contributors is insufficient.
- **E — Social efficacy:** the representation has a documented role in legitimation, recognition, credibility allocation, institutional action, or repeated practice. A single phrase without a demonstrable practical or institutional role does not establish this condition.

The diagnostic form is:

\[
\boxed{\text{Ideological fetishism} \Rightarrow S \land O \land E}
\]

The exact technical warrant is a necessary descriptive baseline, but it is not itself evidence of ideology. The three requirements are analytically distinct. Scope extension concerns what the credential is made to warrant; objectal displacement concerns where the authority appears to reside; social efficacy concerns what the representation does in practice.

Fantasy explains how the conjunction can appear as a scene of closure. Disavowal is tested separately and is not required for the core diagnosis.

### 4.3 Rival explanations and counter-evidence

The following alternatives must be examined before a case is diagnosed:
- accurate shorthand whose context preserves the technical scope;
- genre conventions and headline compression;
- explicit qualifications elsewhere in the same communication chain;
- justified reliance on a trusted kernel or expert testimony;
- visible semantic review and human validation;
- institutional endorsement that accurately reports the scope of the check;
- correction, clarification, or withdrawal after a discrepancy is identified;
- technical reuse that does not involve theorem-level reliance.

A case should be classified as warranted reliance, transparent mediation, ambiguous representation, or insufficient evidence when S, O, or E is not established. This is what makes the concept discriminating rather than universal.
## 5. Method and Case Selection

### 5.1 Unit of analysis

The unit of analysis is a dated public or institutional claim that invokes Lean verification as part of the warrant for a mathematical result, paired with:
- the exact formal proposition and proof artifact;
- the formal environment, assumptions, dependencies, and checking status;
- the intended mathematical claim;
- the specification relation between the formal proposition and that claim;
- the human, technical, and institutional mediation;
- the surrounding communication, qualifications, and subsequent responses.

A headline is not analyzed in isolation from the article, linked technical materials, qualifications, and corrections.

### 5.2 Comparative design

The four cases were selected as a purposive, maximum-variation set. They differ in production history, relation between formal artifact and natural-language exposition, visibility of human review, and institutional setting. They are not a representative sample of Lean use and are not matched controls.

The cases have distinct analytical functions:
1. **Riemann hypothesis:** formal certification, institutional authorization, AI-mediated exposition, and a public dispute about the meaning of a certified derivation.
2. **Navier–Stokes:** the relation between a Lean formalization and a separate natural-language proof.
3. **Erdős / AlphaProof Nexus:** proof search, benchmark scope, human statement validation, and artifact provenance.
4. **Liquid Tensor Experiment:** explicit semantic sanity checks and visible collective mathematical labour as a counter-case.

### 5.3 Source selection and inference limits

The evidence cut-off is 28 November 2026. Sources include official institutional statements, primary research papers, formal repositories and pinned commits, independent technical critiques, verification reports, and project documentation. Independent audits are distinguished from primary claims and from our own source-level inspection.

The study does not claim to have independently rebuilt every repository or adjudicated the underlying mathematics of the four cases. Where a claim rests on a source-level comparison, this is stated explicitly. Public documents establish representations and documented practices; they do not, by themselves, establish population-level audience belief or unconscious motives. No interviews or public-trust survey are conducted.

## 6. Four Lean Cases

### 6.1 Riemann hypothesis: institutional certification and mathematical reconstruction

On 20 November 2026, Levent Alpöge released a note and a large Lean development concerning the Riemann hypothesis and the generalized Riemann hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions (Alpöge 2026). The Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative issued a joint statement on 21 November, expressing “full confidence” in the proof and stating that the community certified both the formal statement and the proof. The formalized Riemann-hypothesis statement says that every zero of Mathlib’s Riemann zeta function, other than the trivial zeros and the excluded point \(s=1\), has real part \(1/2\). The exclusion of \(s=1\) matters because Mathlib assigns a conventional value to its formalized zeta function at the classical pole (Mathlib Documentation 2026; Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative 2026).

The certification rests on several distinct checks: expert vetting of the formal statement; successful compilation of the archive; comparison of the proved theorem with the Mathlib statement using independent tools; and acceptance of the exported proof by Lean’s kernel and additional checkers. Andrew Sutherland’s verification record reports 175,187 Lean source files and 17,374,215 lines, successful compilation of all 175,187 modules, and replay through Lean’s kernel and independently implemented checkers. The five headline theorems depend only on Lean’s three standard axioms, and independent statement-comparison tools confirm that the exported theorems match the challenge statements (Sutherland 2026a). The original archive and the shorter rewrite released on 25 November remain distinct source objects; the latter has its own verification record (Sutherland 2026b).

The result’s public afterlife quickly extended beyond institutional certification. On 24 November, Anurag Sahay, Will Sawin, and Lior Silberman proposed the G.R.H. Polymath project. Its stated goals include coordinating work already underway, obtaining a human-readable proof of RH and GRH, clarifying the relevant literature, and investigating mathematical advances and generalizations (Sahay, Sawin, and Silberman 2026). This is direct evidence of mathematical-community participation, not merely the repetition of a public announcement.

Several efforts to translate the formal archive into conventional mathematical exposition are also underway. Michael Rubinstein’s Waterloo page provides a 45-page outline of the shortened proof and a 265-page working guide to the original proof. The page explicitly identifies both as works in progress: the shortened outline leaves a key zero-density estimate outlined rather than proved, while the longer guide reduces the Riemann hypothesis to thirteen classical hypotheses that are not all proved in the document (Rubinstein 2026). Jimmie Adriazola’s reconstruction project contains a 297-page reconstruction of the original archive, a 117-page working manuscript, and a 59-page RH-specific version; it maps manuscript passages to Lean declarations but labels the work incomplete, conditional, and not independently verified (Adriazola 2026). The separate rh-lean-roadmap project offers a 497-page main text, 963 pages of longer proofs, and a 122-page declaration index. Its documentation states that several AI models generated the exposition from the Lean source and cross-checked it, so it is an AI-mediated exposition rather than a conventional independently authored proof paper (rh-lean-roadmap 2026).

The community repository also preserves the original release, earlier cleanup history, and a readable refactor in separate branches. Its generated contributor record lists 192 accounts and 6,079 merged-PR counts, including earlier Alpöge history. These figures document repository maintenance and refactoring, not theorem authorship or 6,079 independent mathematical contributions (Leanprover Community 2026). A non-peer-reviewed preprint by Parker Emmerson challenges the epistemic and foundational significance of the certification while granting the existence of the Lean derivation and reported checks. It disputes what follows from the derivation and the role of its foundational assumptions; it does not report a Lean kernel rejection or establish that the Riemann hypothesis is false (Emmerson 2026).

**Žižekian assessment.** **The symbolic order and the subject position.** The Lean FRO/Mathlib statement operates within the symbolic order of mathematical proof norms: statement identity, compilation, kernel checking, independent replay, and expert review are the recognized procedures through which this result becomes certifiable. The institutions issuing the statement are not the Lacanian Big Other; they occupy authorized positions within that symbolic order. The experts and checkers are presented as subjects who know how to assess the formal statement and its proof. The certificate itself is not a subject-supposed-to-know, but a credential that condenses the outcome of these authorized procedures.

**Fantasy, practical belief, and warrant-scope transfer.** The phrase “full confidence” can stage a scene of epistemic closure, but the statement specifies the checks on which that confidence rests. The Polymath project and the ongoing human-readable reconstructions show that mathematical interpretation remains active after certification. The public record therefore does not establish a transfer from the kernel’s local warrant to an unqualified guarantee of every mathematical interpretation. Nor does it show practical belief in a self-sufficient machine guarantee: the visible practices continue to rely on experts, statement comparison, and mathematical reconstruction. The certification has institutional efficacy as an act of recognition, but this is not E in the diagnostic sense: the record does not show that an overextended warrant representation produced the recognition. Objectal displacement and fetishistic disavowal are not established. The case is best understood as **institutional authorization with visible mediation**, not as a demonstrated instance of ideological fetishism.

### 6.2 Navier–Stokes: contested correspondence and direct technical integration

On 8 September 2026, OpenAI announced a proposed solution to the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem, presenting an analytical proof together with a Lean formalization. The public repository’s first commit, dated 8 September, contained the initial formal artifact. A second commit on 10 September changed 188 files, adding 19,388 lines and deleting 79. The cert-machine audit reports that the initial commit did not yet discharge the energy-bound and positive-time force-support obligations; the 10 September commit added ActualCandidate.lean and Theorem.lean, supplying the missing witnesses and the full theorem statement (OpenAI 2026; OpenAI NavierStokesAndEuler 2026; Toledo 2026a).

This repository correction must be distinguished from the later paper-to-Lean discrepancies. Bastounis, Circelli, and Hansen identify a mismatch in Lemma 8.6: the written inverse estimate requires \(m+4\) input derivatives, whereas the corresponding Lean estimate uses \(m+5\). They also identify differences between the paper’s pressure-flux estimate and the corresponding Lean bound and proof route (Bastounis et al. 2026). The two files central to these examples—SmoothFamilyTorusInverse.lean and R3/PressureFlux.lean—are byte-identical between the 8 and 10 September commits. Thus the later commit closes the initial artifact-completeness gap but does not correct the intermediate estimates identified in the independent comparison. OpenAI acknowledged the paper-to-code mismatch in its response to New Scientist and said that errors in the written proof would be corrected as they were found (New Scientist 2026).

Other audits address different questions. The cert-machine audit records the initial completeness gap, the subsequent correction, and the relation between the main formal statement and the relevant Clay alternatives, but does not issue a verdict on the mathematical correctness of the proof. The SafeVerifyAgent audit reports no load-bearing weakening or kernel defect within the portions it examined; its coverage record also states that a substantial part of the estimate-heavy proof remains unread or only mechanically screened. It is therefore a partial AI-assisted audit, not a complete independent human mathematical review (Toledo 2026a; SafeVerifyAgent 2026).

The formal artifact has nevertheless entered several downstream technical projects. The ns-formalization-benchmark project compares the OpenAI source with a LeanPool port, pinning the source and Mathlib versions and recording a per-module SHA-256 provenance manifest. It builds the same two target theorems in a common Lean 4.34.0-rc2 and Mathlib environment. The reported clean builds take 18 minutes 4 seconds for the OpenAI source and 11 minutes 11 seconds for LeanPool; aggregate peak compiler memory is 13.273 GiB and 6.451 GiB, respectively. The benchmark reports a 1.62-fold speed-up and a 51.4% reduction in aggregate peak memory for the LeanPool version. This is direct artifact reuse and reproducible build comparison, not independent confirmation that the formal statements are semantically equivalent to the informal Clay problem (Vilin97 2026).

KTS Global has also published a Lean 4.34.1 port and an R2/R3 community-verification package. Its README explicitly states that the mathematics and formalization are inherited from OpenAI, and that the port is not an independent mathematical proof or a claim of Clay recognition. The Tengoku repository incorporates the LeanPool/Navier–Stokes material into a larger formal library and preserves upstream attribution in its NOTICE and source metadata (KTS Global 2026; Tengoku contributors 2026). These are further forms of technical integration and archival stabilization.

**Žižekian assessment.** **The subject position and the Big Other.** OpenAI’s announcement assigns its AI system the position of mathematical discoverer, while the accompanying paper, formalization, repository, and subsequent audits distribute the knowing work across researchers, proof-search systems, Lean, and external reviewers. The AI system is thus publicly attributed a solver position; the available material does not establish a Lacanian transference relation in which the mathematical community supposes it to possess complete knowledge of the problem. The Big Other is the symbolic order of mathematical standards and recognition, enacted through the Millennium problem’s formulation, proof norms, peer criticism, and institutional procedures—not OpenAI, Lean, or the Clay Institute considered as an individual authority.

**Warrant-scope transfer and practical belief.** The paper-to-Lean discrepancies identify a gap between particular written estimates and formal declarations. They do not show that the public announcement transferred the entire warrant of the natural-language proof to Lean: the announcement presents a written proof and a formalization, and the subsequent technical reuse is directed to porting, benchmarking, and auditing. The public acknowledgement of discrepancies is evidence that the gap entered the discursive record; the benchmark and porting projects do not establish that users nevertheless treated the Lean credential as a complete guarantee. Accordingly, the case supplies a concrete **candidate specification gap**, but S (warrant-scope transfer), O (objectal displacement), and practical fetishistic disavowal remain unestablished. The announcement and response have public effects in attracting scrutiny and organizing technical activity. These effects are not E in the diagnostic sense, because the record does not establish that an overextended credential representation produced legitimation, credibility allocation, or institutional action.

### 6.3 Erdős problems and AlphaProof Nexus: benchmark recognition, statement correction, and distributed attribution

Tsoukalas et al. (2026) report that AlphaProof Nexus solved nine of 353 formalized Erdős statements attempted and 44 of 492 OEIS conjectures. The nine rows in the Science paper’s table do not represent nine distinct, unqualified original Erdős problems. They cover seven problem numbers: two parts of #12, two parts of #741, a variant of #138, and a more general variant of #26, alongside #125, #152, and #846. The #26 item is a Tenenbaum variant of a Behrend-type question and is explicitly marked as a more general variant that was not itself posed by Erdős. The Science table does not mark #125 as a variant; its density qualification is a separate clarification of the intended statement. The paper’s abstract uses the compact phrase “nine Erdős problems,” while its table marks the parts and variants.

The pinned AlphaProof results archive contains nine files under APNOutputs/ErdosProblems (Google DeepMind 2026). A source-level inspection found no explicit sorry, admit, or axiom token in those files; this is distinct from a full build and from semantic comparison with the original historical questions. A further provenance issue concerns #741(i). The archive file erdos_741.parts.i.lean formalizes the natural-density reading and gives a counterexample, whereas the Science table counts the corrected upper-density version. Formal Conjectures PR #3778 records the upper-density variant as solved and attributes its discovery to the DeepMind prover agent. The Science paper explains that, for #125 and #741(i), the interpretation of “density” was corrected after the agent found proofs under the natural-density reading; the agent then resolved the corrected lower- and upper-density questions. The natural-density counterexample and the upper-density theorem are different mathematical statements (Google DeepMind Formal Conjectures 2026a; Erdős Problems Research Library 2026).

The paper reports that experts checked, after each solve, that the Lean statement faithfully captured the original conjecture. Its contribution statement also identifies human work in problem selection, statement design, validation, Lean formalization, natural-language proof writing, repository coordination, and research direction. The prover agent is assigned a proof-search and discovery role, but the overall research output is not attributed to a single autonomous system. The heavily reviewed Formal Conjectures PR #4384, with 53 commits and 135 comments, concerns integration and review of OEIS outputs, not the nine Erdős statements; its event counts must not be transferred to the Erdős sample. An external audit reports replaying ten proof items through Lean and nanoda while treating statement correspondence as a separate question (Toledo 2026b).

Within the bounded scholarly and repository search, no later mathematical paper or formal development was located that uses one of the nine Science-table Erdős results as a premise in a new theorem. Their formal archive and variant-specific status records establish local recognition and reproducibility, not theorem-level assimilation.

A separate AlphaProof Nexus output has a clearer research afterlife and must not be conflated with the nine Erdős statements. The system discovered a result on monochromatic quantum graphs. Krenn et al. (2026) translate the formal discovery into a tensor-algebraic argument and derive a physical no-go result for generating high-dimensional photonic GHZ states with the specified resources. Their paper explicitly describes the AlphaProof result as “human-digested” in the manuscript. The theorem in the AlphaProof archive concerns even \(N\geq4\) with \(D=N\); the Krenn paper extends the result to \(D\geq N\). Subsequent work by Kenta Kitamura and Rishi Rajasekaran develops related bounds. Rajasekaran’s separate all-orders Lean proof establishes a result for even \(N\geq6\) and \(D\geq3\), using the same formal target definitions; it is a distinct AI-assisted proof project, not an AlphaProof output. The public attribution records for that later proof differ between the Krenn preprint and Formal Conjectures PR #6627, and are retained as separate records rather than harmonized (Krenn et al. 2026; Rajasekaran 2026; Google DeepMind Formal Conjectures 2026b).

**Žižekian assessment.** **The subject-supposed-to-know and distributed labour.** The paper attributes proof-search and discovery to AlphaProof Nexus, thereby assigning the agent a public solver position. That attribution is bounded by the same paper’s account of expert statement validation and its contribution statement, which identifies human work in problem selection, formalization, validation, natural-language proof writing, repository coordination, and research direction. The agent can therefore be analyzed as occupying an attributed knowing position in the public narrative; the evidence does not establish that it is treated as an omniscient subject or that a transferential relation has formed around it. The relevant Big Other is the symbolic order of mathematical problem statements, proof standards, expert validation, and publication—not the agent or DeepMind as an empirical institution.

**Warrant-scope transfer and practical belief.** The expression “nine Erdős problems” compresses a heterogeneous set of parts and variants, but the Science table identifies the parts and marks the relevant variants, and the paper reports expert validation. This is achievement-label compression, not evidence that Lean’s warrant for a formal proof was transferred to a wider mathematical claim. The #741 density issue is a statement-provenance and target-identification problem; it does not show that an invalid proof was accepted. The later quantum-graph work demonstrates human translation and subsequent mathematical/physical use of a separate AlphaProof result, not reliance on the nine Erdős credentials. The available record establishes publication and benchmark recognition, but not S, O, or E as conditions of an ideological transfer, and not fetishistic practical belief. AlphaProof is therefore a case of **distributed discovery and attribution**, with an AI solver position that remains embedded in human validation and mathematical interpretation.

### 6.4 Liquid Tensor Experiment: semantic auditability and methodological diffusion

The Liquid Tensor Experiment (LTE) began with Peter Scholze’s challenge and was completed in July 2022 (Scholze 2021; Mathlib Community 2022). The original lean-liquid repository is a Lean 3 project pinned to Lean 3.48.0 and a Mathlib3 revision. Its formal development has two principal targets: first_target, combining aspects of Analytic 9.4 and 9.5, and liquid_tensor_experiment, formalizing Clausen–Scholze Theorem 1.1. The latter states that, for a profinite set \(S\), a \(p\)-Banach space \(V\), and exponents \(0<p'<p\leq1\), the higher Ext groups vanish: \(\operatorname{Ext}^{i}_{\mathrm{Cond}(\mathrm{Ab})}(M_{p'}(S),V)=0\) for every \(i\geq1\). The project’s source defines condensed objects through sheaves on the Profinite category with the pro-étale topology; source comments also note that the formal implementation is, strictly, pyknotic while intended to serve the corresponding condensed role in Lean’s type theory (Leanprover Community 2022; Commelin and Topaz 2024).

The project’s public organization made the relationship between mathematical exposition and formal declarations unusually visible. The Blueprint served as a guide for mathematicians without Lean expertise, a map of dependencies, and a shared route between informal statements and Lean declarations. The repository census records 57 pull requests—49 merged, six closed unmerged, and two open—and 15 PR-author accounts. These are observable measures of public collaboration, not a complete count of mathematical labour. Adam Topaz’s discussion of definitions addresses the semantic question directly: definitions may be formally consistent yet fail to capture the intended mathematical objects. The project therefore developed examples and sanity checks designed to be readable by mathematicians with limited Lean experience, allowing them to inspect expected properties of the definitions (Topaz 2022).

The later mathematical and formalization record shows several distinct forms of uptake. The underlying condensed and analytic mathematics continues to develop, but such mathematical applications cannot be attributed to the Lean formalization without a documented dependency on the formal artifact. Mathlib4’s Condensed.Basic uses a different implementation, defining condensed objects as sheaves on compact Hausdorff spaces with the coherent topology; it is not a verified port of the LTE Lean 3 theorem or proof code. The Atlas Lean project imports Mathlib4 condensed infrastructure for its development of condensed topological rings, again without importing the original LTE theorem (Mathlib Contributors 2026; Atlas Lean Contributors 2026).

The strongest direct downstream uptake concerns method rather than theorem code. Becker et al.’s 2026 paper on the Lean formalization of Carleson’s theorem explicitly traces the strategy of proving expected properties of definitions as semantic sanity checks back at least to LTE. It also describes the use of a Blueprint to keep the informal mathematical structure and formal proof aligned (Becker et al. 2026). Commelin and Topaz’s analysis of abstraction boundaries and spec-driven development further documents the methodological significance of LTE as a collaborative formalization project (Commelin and Topaz 2024). No later project was located that directly imports the original LTE target theorem or its Lean 3 proof declarations.

**Žižekian assessment.** **The Big Other and the knowing subject.** LTE is organized around a shared symbolic order of mathematical definitions, theorem statements, explanatory standards, and formal proof obligations. The Blueprint makes these norms and the relations among them visible; neither the Blueprint nor Lean is itself the Big Other. Mathematical contributors and subject experts occupy differentiated knowing positions: formalizers implement definitions and proofs, while domain mathematicians inspect examples and expected properties. The formal credential is therefore not presented as an autonomous subject-supposed-to-know. It is a check within a publicly inspectable division of epistemic labour.

**Practical belief and objectal displacement.** The examples folder and definition checks are not merely declarations that semantic correctness matters; they are repeatable practices through which contributors externalize and test their expectations about the mathematical objects. The Blueprint similarly stores a shared, revisable account of the intended argument and its formal dependencies. In Žižekian terms, epistemic commitments are materially organized in these procedures rather than concentrated in the machine certificate. This does not mean the project is free of symbolic authority or that every participant understands every definition. It means the evidence shows a counter-practice to objectal displacement: the warrant of the formal result remains connected to inspectable mathematical interpretation. This supports an analogy with belief externalized in practice, not a direct inference about the participants’ subjective belief. The later Carleson project’s explicit acknowledgement of this method supports methodological diffusion, not a causal claim that transparency produces trust. LTE therefore provides evidence of **practice-based epistemic commitments organized through shared semantic checks**. This is compatible with Žižek’s account of belief externalized in practice, but it does not by itself establish the stronger fetishistic mechanism; S, O, and E as conditions of ideological transfer are not established.
## 7. Comparative Discussion: Recognition, Subject Positions, and the Scope of the Credential

The four cases show that formal verification does not produce one uniform form of authority. The same technical resource—a machine-checked formal proof—enters different symbolic orders, divisions of epistemic labour, and practices of recognition. The comparative task is therefore not to rank the cases by how much they “trust Lean,” but to identify what the credential is made to warrant, where the knowing position is located, and how the relevant mathematical community sustains or contests that attribution.

### 7.1 Warrant-scope transfer: a discriminating comparison

Warrant-scope transfer is not synonymous with publicity, institutional recognition, an inaccurate headline, or a discrepancy between two texts. It names a specific non-deductive attribution: the warrant supplied by checking a formal proposition is made to support a broader intended mathematical claim, or claims about its significance or settled status, without an adequately supported specification and interpretive bridge. The three diagnostic conditions must therefore be evaluated separately.

| Case | S: Warrant-scope transfer | O: Objectal displacement | E: Social efficacy of the alleged transfer | Other documented social effects | Evidential judgement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Riemann | Not established; the certification identifies statement review, compilation, comparison, and independent checking | Not established; the relations supporting certification remain visible | Not established for an overextended warrant representation | Institutional certification and public authorization are documented | Layered certification with continuing mathematical interpretation |
| Navier–Stokes | Candidate specification gap, but transfer from the written proof to the Lean credential is not established | Not established; public announcement presents both a written proof and a formalization | Not established; no legitimating effect of an overextended credential representation is shown | Public attention, scrutiny, and technical activity are documented independently of such a transfer | Strongest candidate for testing the boundary of the credential, not a completed diagnosis |
| Erdős / AlphaProof | Not established; “nine Erdős problems” is achievement-label compression, while the table records parts and variants | Not established; expert statement validation and distributed human contributions are reported | Not established for an overextended proof-warrant representation | Publication and benchmark recognition are documented | Distributed discovery and attribution, with a separate quantum-graph afterlife |
| Liquid Tensor Experiment | Not established; the project explicitly connects formal statements with mathematical checks | Counter-evidence: semantic checks and Blueprint keep mediation inspectable | Not established for an overextended warrant representation | Community collaboration and methodological uptake are documented | Transparent mediation and shared epistemic practice |

The table does not code “not established” as proof of absence. It records the evidential status of each condition in the bounded four-case dossier. The fifth column records social effects that are real but do not satisfy E for an ideological transfer. Institutional recognition, publicity, technical reuse, and methodological diffusion may occur without a demonstrated overextended warrant representation. Likewise, a specification discrepancy can be real without the credential having been used to warrant a broader claim.

Riemann illustrates institutional authorization without demonstrated warrant-scope transfer. The joint statement’s “full confidence” is consequential because it is issued through recognized mathematical institutions, but the statement also specifies expert vetting, compilation, theorem comparison, and independent checking. The symbolic authority of certification is therefore visible as an institutional act within mathematical norms; the record does not show that this act displaced the supporting procedures or made the kernel a self-sufficient guarantor.

Navier–Stokes presents the most consequential specification dispute. The difference between the written estimates and particular Lean declarations identifies a gap in correspondence. Yet the public announcement presents an analytical write-up and a formalization, while later projects use the artifact for porting, benchmarking, auditing, and library integration. Those activities establish technical integration, not a transfer of the proof’s entire mathematical warrant to the Lean credential. The available evidence therefore supports a candidate for S, but not a completed finding of S, O, and E as an ideological conjunction.

AlphaProof requires a further distinction. The phrase “nine Erdős problems” compresses a table containing separate parts and variants; the #125 density clarification is not itself a table-designated variant. This is a question of achievement description and statement provenance, not automatically a transfer of proof warrant. The paper’s report of expert validation and its contribution statement make human mediation visible. The separate quantum-graph result has a documented afterlife in human mathematical exposition and physical interpretation, but that does not establish reliance on the nine Erdős credentials.

LTE supplies a counter-pattern. Its formal statements were accompanied by a Blueprint, examples, and checks of expected properties of definitions. The formal proof was not presented as eliminating the need to inspect the intended mathematical objects; semantic scrutiny was built into the work. This does not establish that every participant understood every detail, but it does show that formal verification can be embedded in a practice that keeps the specification relation visible.

### 7.2 Ideological fantasy and practical belief: where is closure staged and belief located?

Žižek’s account of practical belief shifts the question from what individual actors say they believe to how belief is sustained in social activity. In the commodity-fetishism example, subjects may know theoretically that money expresses social relations while continuing to treat it in practice as if it possessed value autonomously. The relevant belief is externalized in objects, procedures, and repeated relations; it need not appear as an explicit false proposition in anyone’s mind (Žižek 1989, pp. 28–32).

This distinction matters for the four cases. An ideological fantasy is not simply an optimistic phrase, a benchmark headline, or an institutional statement of confidence. It is a symbolic scenario that makes a wider field of uncertainty appear coherently closed and organizes how actors relate to that field. Repository imports, citations, successful builds, benchmark comparisons, and formal status labels are practices, but they do not by themselves establish fetishistic belief. Their epistemic function must be identified before they can be interpreted ideologically.

In Riemann, “full confidence” can function rhetorically as a scene of closure, but the same statement makes the procedures supporting that confidence explicit, and the subsequent Polymath and reconstruction work keeps mathematical interpretation open. The record therefore supports a public scene of certification, not a demonstrated fantasy that the certificate ends all relevant mathematical work. In Navier–Stokes, the public announcement of a proposed solution alongside a formalization creates a possible closure scene; the written proof, formal artifact, later discrepancies, and independent audits prevent us from concluding that the credential itself supplied a final guarantee. In AlphaProof, the “nine Erdős problems” benchmark formulation can compress a heterogeneous achievement set, but the table, statement corrections, and expert-validation account preserve distinctions that resist a simple fantasy of machine omniscience. In LTE, the Blueprint and definition checks make the relation between formal declarations and mathematical interpretation revisable and inspectable; this is a counter-practice to a fantasy of self-sufficient verification, not proof that all symbolic authority disappears.

In Riemann, certification is followed by Polymath coordination and attempts to reconstruct a human-readable proof. These practices sustain confidence in a formally checked result while continuing to investigate its mathematical exposition. The record does not show that the credential is treated as a substitute for interpretation. The relevant practical belief is distributed across checking, expert assessment, and reconstruction, rather than demonstrably concentrated in the certificate.

In Navier–Stokes, technical reuse persists alongside criticism of paper-to-Lean correspondence. This coexistence is not sufficient to establish the “nevertheless” structure of fetishistic disavowal. The downstream projects reuse the code for benchmarking, porting, auditing, and integration; the record does not show that they rely on the disputed written estimates as if the Lean credential had settled them. A practical-belief diagnosis would require evidence that actors acknowledge the correspondence gap yet continue to act as though the credential guaranteed the broader claim. That evidence is not presently established.

In AlphaProof, the public attribution of discovery to an agent is accompanied by expert validation, human formalization, and natural-language proof work. This is not evidence that participants believe the agent knows everything or that the machine’s output is accepted without mediation. The public narrative gives the agent a discovery role, while the research practice distributes validation and interpretation among human contributors. The distinction between attributed solver status and practical belief in an autonomous guarantor must be maintained.

LTE offers the clearest evidence of **practice-based epistemic commitments organized through shared semantic checks**. The examples folder and definition checks make expectations about mathematical objects inspectable; the Blueprint preserves a revisable relation between informal mathematics and formal declarations. These procedures externalize epistemic commitments in a way compatible with Žižek’s account of practical belief, while keeping the mediating relations open to scrutiny. This is evidence of a non-fetishistic counter-practice, not a direct inference about participants’ subjective beliefs or proof that every participant understands every definition.

The comparison therefore yields a conditional conclusion. Practical belief is not inherently ideological. It becomes relevant to fetishism only when a credential is practically treated as carrying a broader guarantee while the relations that delimit its warrant are acknowledged yet rendered ineffective in the relevant practice. The four cases do not establish that full pattern.

### 7.3 Subject positions: who is supposed to know?

The Lacanian subject-supposed-to-know is a position attributed within a transferential relation; it is not a synonym for trust, expertise, computational competence, or institutional reputation (Lacan 1977, pp. 230–35). The four cases show different public distributions of knowing positions, but they do not provide evidence sufficient to diagnose a psychoanalytic transference relation in the mathematical community. The concept is used here to analyze attribution, not to make a clinical claim about researchers or audiences.

In Riemann, experts, formal reviewers, and independent checkers are publicly positioned as those who can assess the statement and proof. The credential condenses the result of their work, but it is not itself the knowing subject. The subsequent reconstruction projects also position mathematical expositors as responsible for rendering the proof intelligible.

In Navier–Stokes, OpenAI’s public narrative attributes discovery to its AI system, giving the system a solver position. The proof paper, Lean development, and external audits nevertheless distribute knowledge work across researchers, formal tools, and reviewers. The available record shows an attributed AI-discoverer position, not that the community supposes the system to possess complete or unchallengeable knowledge.

In AlphaProof, the agent is explicitly assigned proof-search and discovery achievements. That subject position is qualified by the paper’s report of expert statement validation and by its account of human work in problem selection, formalization, validation, exposition, and research direction. The agent is a publicly attributed solver within a collaborative research arrangement, not an autonomous subject that exhausts the knowledge of the result.

In LTE, the knowing positions are differentiated across formalizers, domain mathematicians, and maintainers. Formalizers construct the Lean declarations and proof terms; mathematicians inspect definitions, examples, and the intended argument; the Blueprint coordinates these roles. No single machine or human is represented as the exclusive bearer of mathematical knowledge.

These differences matter for attribution and authority. The subject-supposed-to-know is not automatically the AI agent in AI-assisted mathematics, nor the expert in a formalization project. It is a position that must be shown to be assigned and sustained in a particular relation. The current cases support a comparison of attributed knowing roles, but not a claim that a full transferential relation has been empirically demonstrated.

### 7.4 The Big Other: symbolic authorization without a final guarantor

The Big Other names the symbolic locus of language, law, norms, and recognition. It is not identical with a particular institution, journal, repository, software package, or mathematical community. Such entities can enact or administer symbolic rules, but none is the Big Other as an empirical object. The absence of an “Other of the Other” means that no final symbolic authority can guarantee the consistency of the entire order from outside it; it does not imply that mathematical proof is arbitrary or that local standards are ineffective (Lacan 2019, ch. XVI; Žižek 1989, Part II).

The Riemann case shows how recognized proof norms and institutional procedures authorize a result, while reconstruction and public criticism keep the question of mathematical interpretation open. The Navier–Stokes case shows how the Millennium problem’s statement, standards of analysis, peer criticism, and publication practices frame the dispute over what has been established. AlphaProof’s authority is situated within mathematical problem registers, formal statement conventions, expert validation, and publication. LTE’s Blueprint and semantic checks make the shared norms connecting definitions, statements, and proofs unusually explicit.

The Big Other therefore helps explain why a credential can carry symbolic authority: it is legible within an order that recognizes formal checking, mathematical expertise, and institutional validation. But it cannot supply a final guarantee that every level of the result is settled. The ideological question arises only if the credential is made to appear as though it could provide that guarantee. In the present cases, this is a diagnostic possibility rather than an established general practice.

### 7.5 Comparative findings and the article’s contribution

The comparison yields four findings.

First, **genuine technical reliability can become a bearer of wider symbolic authority, but it does not do so automatically**. The credibility of a Lean credential depends on real checking achievements; the ideological question concerns what further claims are attached to those achievements.

Second, **institutional recognition and social mediation are not themselves fetishism**. Riemann shows consequential institutional certification with visible expert and technical mediation. AlphaProof shows agent attribution within a distributed contribution structure. LTE shows how a formal credential can remain connected to inspectable semantic work.

Third, **technical integration, achievement compression, and theorem-level reliance must remain distinct from warrant-scope transfer**. Navier–Stokes supplies a concrete correspondence problem and a possible site for S, but the public record does not establish that the Lean credential was used as a self-sufficient guarantee of the entire written proof. AlphaProof’s “nine Erdős problems” wording concerns the description of achievements and variants; it is not by itself a transfer of proof warrant. The downstream use of code in a benchmark or library does not establish mathematical dependence on the theorem.

Fourth, **the negative and counter-cases are constitutive of the theory’s explanatory value**. If every use of formal verification were classified as fetishistic, the concept would cease to distinguish transparent mediation from objectal displacement. LTE shows that the formal credential can be embedded in a practice that externalizes epistemic commitments through shared examples, definition checks, and a revisable Blueprint, without thereby establishing a fetishistic belief structure.

On the present evidence, no case establishes the full conjunction (S\land O\land E). Riemann and AlphaProof show institutional and benchmark recognition with visible human mediation; Navier–Stokes is the strongest candidate for a specification gap but does not establish practical disavowal; LTE provides a counter-practice in which semantic mediation remains inspectable. The article therefore offers a **conditional diagnostic theory**, not a claim that the four cases collectively prove the existence of a generalized ideology of machine verification.

The originality claim remains deliberately bounded. Vallverdú’s Locus Thesis supplies the closest recent account of the component structure of mathematical warrant; Kallel and El Louadi analyze the shift from derivational verification to human adjudication; López-Corona examines human-machine assemblages and distributed mathematical labour; Erfani, Hourigan, and Omodeo address ideological or political dimensions of technology and knowledge. Thomas et al. (2018) offer a particularly close account of algorithms as fetishes: their ethnography analyzes how technical capacities, social relations, labour, and broader promises become materially condensed in algorithmic objects. The present article does not claim to discover algorithmic fetishism, distributed labour, software-mediated trust, or ideology critique of technology. Its narrower contribution is to identify a specific epistemic operation: the transfer of a genuinely warranted local check to a wider mathematical claim, the displacement of the relations supporting that claim into the “Lean-verified” credential, and the requirement that this displacement have documented social efficacy. The distinction is not simply that Lean is more reliable than other algorithms; it is that the object of analysis is a precisely scoped formal warrant and the non-deductive extension of its authority. The phrase “epistemic fetishism” is also used in analytic epistemology for a different position (Ahlstrom-Vij 2021). The article therefore retains the full expression **“the ideological fetishism of formal verification”** and does not claim to have coined the bare phrase.

## 8. Conclusion

What is ideological about a correct proof? Nothing follows from its correctness alone. Lean’s kernel provides a genuine and valuable form of formal assurance. The ideological question begins only when the credential associated with that assurance is made to stand for a wider mathematical authority that the checking operation does not, by itself, establish—and when that enlargement displaces the relations that support the wider claim and has a documented practical or institutional effect.

Drawing on Žižek’s critique of ideology, this article has proposed the concept of the **ideological fetishism of formal verification**. Ideological fantasy describes how a local check may stage the appearance of closure around a wider uncertainty. Fetishism describes how authority generated through technical, semantic, and institutional relations may appear to reside autonomously in the credential “Lean-verified.” Žižek’s account of practical belief directs attention to the practices through which such authority may be sustained, rather than to presumed private convictions. Fetishistic disavowal remains a secondary possibility: it requires evidence that relevant limits are acknowledged yet rendered practically ineffective in a consequential relation of reliance. Lacanian concepts further clarify how symbolic norms authorize a credential and how knowing positions are attributed to human experts, institutions, or AI systems without treating Lean itself as the Big Other or the subject-supposed-to-know.

The four cases do not establish a generalized ideology of machine verification. Riemann shows consequential institutional certification alongside explicit checking procedures and continuing mathematical reconstruction. Navier–Stokes contains a concrete dispute about the correspondence between parts of a written proof and the formal artifact, but the available record does not establish that the Lean credential was treated as a self-sufficient guarantee of the entire written argument. AlphaProof displays benchmark recognition and an attributed AI solver role within a research process that also records expert statement validation and distributed human contributions; the description of nine Erdős results is not, by itself, a transfer of proof warrant. The Liquid Tensor Experiment demonstrates how formalization can be organized around inspectable definitions, examples, and a revisable Blueprint, keeping semantic mediation visible.

The comparative analysis therefore distinguishes three conditions for an ideological diagnosis: **S**, warrant-scope transfer; **O**, objectal displacement; and **E**, the social efficacy of that alleged transfer. Institutional recognition, public attention, technical integration, and methodological diffusion are important social effects, but they do not automatically satisfy E. On the present evidence, no case establishes the full conjunction \(S\land O\land E\). Navier–Stokes remains the strongest candidate for examining a specification gap, not a demonstrated instance of ideological transfer. Riemann and AlphaProof show forms of recognition with visible mediation, while Liquid Tensor supplies a counter-practice in which semantic checks remain part of the formalization process.

The article’s contribution is consequently conditional and diagnostic rather than universal. It does not argue that formal verification is inherently fetishistic, that rational reliance on Lean is an ideological error, or that mathematical truth is reducible to social recognition. It explains how a real and bounded technical guarantee may, under identifiable conditions, become a symbolic bearer of broader authority. The task of ideology critique is not to expose the machine as false. It is to determine when the authority of a correct check is made to warrant more than it establishes, when the relations supporting that authority disappear behind the credential, and when this displacement matters in practice.
